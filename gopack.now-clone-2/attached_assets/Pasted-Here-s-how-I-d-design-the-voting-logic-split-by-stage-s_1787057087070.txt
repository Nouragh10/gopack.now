Here's how I'd design the voting logic — split by stage, since each one has a different failure mode if left naive.

**1. Destination voting**
- Resolution: majority wins once all members lock in (not unanimity — you already handle dissent gracefully with the "note" pattern).
- Tie-break: if it's genuinely split (e.g., 2v2), don't auto-pick — surface it back to the group with a "no majority yet, break the tie" prompt rather than silently defaulting to the host's choice. Silently favoring the host undercuts the "democratic" positioning.
- Timeout: if voting stalls (say, 48-72h), host gets an option to force-decide — otherwise trips die in limbo. Log this as a host override, not a natural majority, so you can distinguish "group decided" from "host decided" in analytics.

**2. Accommodation voting**
- Same shape: net-positive swipes wins, but set a minimum participation threshold (e.g., majority of members must have swiped) before locking in — otherwise one fast swiper can decide for a group of 6.

**3. Wishlist — this is the one that actually needs real logic, and it's where your current design has a bug**

Right now: "all wishes are mandatory" + "top 10 sorted by score" are in tension, and neither accounts for trip length or member fairness. Here's what I'd replace it with:

**a. Cap scales with trip length, not a flat number.**
A 3-day trip and a 10-day trip shouldn't both get "top 10." Use something like 1.5–2 guaranteed wish-slots per day. A 3-day trip gets ~5 guaranteed wishes; a 10-day trip can absorb ~15-20. This directly prevents the overcrowding/pacing problem that likely caused your Day 1 bug.

**b. Fairness quota, not pure net-score ranking.**
Pure net-score lets one popular or vocal member's wishes dominate — a group of 6 where one person adds 8 wishes and everyone upvotes them could crowd out quieter members entirely. That undermines "democratic" as a differentiator. Instead:
- Guarantee each member who submitted at least one wish gets their top 1 wish included (by their own ranking or highest net score among their wishes), *before* filling remaining slots by net score across everyone.
- Fill remaining guaranteed slots by net score, member-agnostic.
- This is a round-robin-then-score-fill pattern — one pass for fairness, one pass for popularity.

**c. Two tiers, not one flat mandatory list.**
Send Claude:
- **Guaranteed** (the capped, fairness-filtered set) — must appear, but Claude still owns *where* they land in the day for pacing/geography.
- **Candidates** (everything else, net-positive but not guaranteed) — Claude can use these as filler if a day has open slots, skip if not.

This also gives Claude an escape valve: if two guaranteed wishes are geographically incompatible for the same day (opposite ends of the city, no way to fit both), it needs a resolution rule — not "force both no matter how bad the pacing." I'd say: Claude flags the conflict in the generation output (e.g., a `conflicts` field noting "X and Y both requested for Day 2, spread across two days instead") rather than silently degrading quality or silently dropping one. That gives you a place to show the group *why* something moved, which also reinforces transparency/trust in the AI layer.

**d. Explicit instruction to preserve chronological/geographic sort regardless of source.**
Whatever caused the Day 1 ordering bug, the underlying principle should be: wishes and AI-picks get merged into one candidate pool *before* generation, not stitched together after — and the final output always gets a single sort-by-time pass applied uniformly, with no code path that appends items post-sort.
